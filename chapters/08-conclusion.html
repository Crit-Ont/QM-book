5  Conclusion

5.1 Summary of the Argument

This book set out to reconstruct quantum mechanics on a probabilistic footing, using the \(\sigma\)-complex formalism of Chapter 1, and then to ask what this reconstruction implies for locality. Three distinct notions of locality have been distinguished:

  • Causal locality (microcausality): the requirement, inherited from special and general relativity, that operators associated with spacelike-separated regions commute (Section 2.1).
  • Bell locality: the statistical independence assumption used in the derivation of the Bell/CHSH inequalities, whose violation by quantum mechanical predictions is confirmed by experiment (Chapter 3).
  • Haag locality: the axiomatic requirement of local quantum field theory that observables be assigned to spacetime regions, together with microcausality and Haag duality (Section 4.7).

The central tension explored is that microcausality and Haag locality both presuppose a fixed spacetime background, while the violation of Bell locality shows that entangled systems are correlated in a way no local common cause can explain. Standard quantum mechanics accommodates this by remaining silent on the mechanism connecting preparation and measurement outcomes, deferring to the Born rule and to an unanalyzed notion of measurement.

The relational/probabilistic reconstruction developed here, together with the discussion of Possibilist Transactional Quantum Theory (PTQT) as an explicit ontological proposal, offers an alternative: entanglement and nonlocal correlation are located not in spacetime but in a pre-spacetime quantum substratum of physical possibilities, from which spacetime events emerge through the actualisation of transactions. On this view, Bell nonlocality is not a violation of causal locality, because the correlations do not require any propagating spacetime influence; and Haag locality, being a spacetime-dependent axiom, cannot be treated as fundamental if the substratum picture is correct.

5.2 What Has Not Been Resolved

This account is deliberately partial. Several difficulties have been identified rather than settled:

  • The reconstruction in terms of \(\sigma\)-complexes and conditional states clarifies the mathematics of state reduction but does not by itself supply a dynamical mechanism for reduction; the treatment of the measurement problem shows what such a mechanism must do, not what it is.
  • The relationship between PTQT and Haag’s axiomatic local quantum physics is one of tension rather than reconciliation: the two frameworks answer different questions about locality, and no unified formalism has been presented that recovers Haag’s local algebras as an emergent, approximate description of the substratum.
  • The status of the quantum substratum itself — whether it should be understood as a further physical domain requiring its own dynamics, or as a heuristic device for speaking about unmeasured possibilities — has been described rather than derived from independent principles.

5.3 Open Questions

The discussion points toward several questions for further work:

  1. Can the emergence of Haag-local spacetime algebras from a pre-spacetime substratum be given a precise mathematical treatment, rather than a conceptual one?
  2. What experimental signatures, if any, would distinguish PTQT-type substratum theories from other interpretations (Bohmian mechanics, many-worlds, relational quantum mechanics) that also reproduce the Bell-violating statistics of standard quantum mechanics?
  3. Does the probabilistic reconstruction extend cleanly to relativistic quantum field theory, where Haag’s framework is best established, or does it require modification at that level?

These questions are not resolved here. The aim of this book has been to make the locality problem in quantum mechanics precise enough that such questions can be posed sharply, and to show that at least one substratum-based account offers a coherent, if incomplete, response to it.
