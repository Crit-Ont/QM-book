Preface

This book is being written as an online work in progress. It examines the relationship between quantum theory and the principle of locality — the idea that objects are only directly influenced by their immediate surroundings. Beginning with the foundational formalism of quantum mechanics, the book does not trace the historical and conceptual development of the Einstein–Podolsky–Rosen (EPR) argument (Einstein et al. (1935)) but considers a formal restatement of quantum theory as a generalised probability theory and then Bell’s theorem (Bell (1964)) as a statement about empirical locality.

The experimental tests of Bell inequalities that followed will be examined only briefly.

While briefly surveying a range of interpretive positions of quantum theory itself, the book focusses on a formulation that is explicitly possibilist. This theory has been developed by Ruth Kastner (Kastner (2022)) but here it is recognised as a theory that includes physics that is not part of standard quantum theory. The reformulation presented here will emphasis possibility, probability and events. This then informs the discussion on nonlocality, realism, and what quantum theory implies about the structure of physical reality.

The book deviates from most presentations in giving weight to an objective probabilistic interpretation of quantum theory, inspired by Kochen (2015) and to the Relativistic Transactional Interpretation of quantum theory (Kastner (2022)). The book will argue that the Relativistic Transactional Interpretation provides the best available account of events and the nonlocal correlations predicted by quantum theory and observed in experiments. The book will also explore the implications of these ideas for our understanding of space, time, and causality.

Ontologically, the main innovation is the enlargement of the notion of reality to include not only actual events but also the potentialities described by the quantum formalism that underpins this actualisation. These potentialities are argued to be not merely epistemic but real in a physical sense, and they can influence actual events in a nonlocal manner. This perspective challenges classical intuitions about the nature of reality and causation, suggesting that the universe is fundamentally interconnected in terms of possibilities.

This ontological implications are taken up in a companion book Existence and Being.
